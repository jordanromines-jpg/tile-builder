# Child development and learning: what should shape Tile Builder

Research notes, 2 Oct 2026. They cover how children aged 3 to 10 build from models and pictures, guided play and
instructions, shapes and maths talk, rewards, screens, attention and failure, and gender. The last section turns them
into design rules for each age band.

## How this was researched, and how far to trust it

- Everything was found by web search. This session's network blocks the journals, PubMed, ERIC and AAP sites, so **no
  paper was opened in full**. Findings come from abstracts and from search summaries of them. Numbers should be
  checked against the original before anyone quotes them outside this repo.
- The session's web-search budget ran out partway through. Questions 1 to 3 are well covered. Questions 4 to 7 are
  partly covered. Where a well-known study could not be checked, it is listed under
  [Not verified in this session](#not-verified-in-this-session) and marked **UNVERIFIED** where it is used. Nothing
  there has a URL, because none was confirmed.
- Strength labels:
  - **Strong**: meta-analysis, or several studies that agree.
  - **Moderate**: one good study, or a few small ones.
  - **Weak**: a small sample, correlation only, or industry practice.
  - **Inference**: our reasoning from the evidence, not a finding.

This file adds to `kids-and-ipad.md`, which covers touch targets, drag-and-drop under 5 and pre-readers.

## The short version

1. **Under 4, a screen alone is not enough.** Copying a block build has been studied from age 4 [4]. Under 4,
   children carry little from a touchscreen to real objects [15][17]. A 3-year-old needs a grown-up building with them.
2. **Step size should follow working memory.** Visual working memory holds about 1.5 items at 5, about 3 at 7 and about
   4 at 10 [18]. So: 1 new tile per step for 3–5, up to 3 for 6–8, up to 4 for 9–10 (inference).
3. **Turning a 3D model is hard for young children.** Mental rotation starts around 4 [12]. Turning a 3D shape in your
   head is hard for most children under 7 [13]. Young kids should see the model from where they sit, not a spinning one.
4. **Guided play beats both free play and plain instruction** for shapes and early maths [23][26]. But teaching one way
   to use a toy makes children explore it less [27]. Each project should end with "change it" and "make your own"
   prompts that ask rather than show.
5. **Expected rewards for doing a task undermine children's own interest** [37][38]. The 2026 AAP policy names
   "intermittent variable reward systems" as a high-risk design [45][46]. The photo of the build is the reward.
   Stickers are a surprise keepsake, not a prize to collect.

## 1. Building from a model or from pictures, by age

### What children can copy

| Age | What the evidence says | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | The Test of Spatial Assembly (TOSA) was made for 3-year-olds. It has 2D trials and 3D trials. A 3D trial asks the child to copy a glued model of **two to four** Mega Bloks with matching loose pieces [1]. In 102 children, TOSA scores predicted maths at age 3, even after vocabulary was taken into account [1]. How complex 3-year-olds' builds were went with their spatial skills then and later, and with their maths at age 3 [3]. Search summaries of the follow-up report that TOSA at 3 explained about half the variance in spatial skill at 4 and 5, and about a third of the variance in maths at 5 [2] (not checked in the original). Children from lower-income homes were already behind in spatial skill by age 3 [2]. | Moderate |
| 4 | Age 4 is treated as the lower limit for testing children on copying block builds [4]. Children aged 4 to 8 still make frequent errors [4]. In a 2D puzzle task, 4-year-olds could mentally turn a shape up to 120°. Younger children were near chance [12]. At about age 4 (mean 3 years 11 months, 116 children), DUPLO copying skill explained about 5% of the variance in early number skills after age, income and vocabulary. The link held for lower-income children, not for better-off ones [5]. | Moderate |
| 5 | On 3D mental rotation with real wooden blocks, 5-year-olds score above chance but mostly guess [13]. Visual working memory holds about 1.5 items [18]. | Moderate |
| 4–8 | Children choose a small set of build orders, and those orders look like adults'. They build **layer by layer**, and their in-between states are more stable than other possible orders [4]. The phrase "considerable proficiency by 8" could not be found in the abstract. The study does show adult-like build orders across 4 to 8, but frequent errors too [4]. | Moderate |
| 6–8 | 6- and 9-year-olds using real toy-maker picture instructions "had difficulty with all but the simplest instructions and assemblies". The 6-year-old girls placed the fewest parts correctly [10] (24 children). A 6-week class programme of LEGO building from picture instructions for 6- and 7-year-olds (409 children against 103 controls) improved spatial skill and maths [8]. In 420 children aged 7 to 9, placing a brick on exactly the right spot was the hardest part. Getting a brick's angle right went with mental rotation [7]. 3D mental rotation is hard for most children under 7. About 57% of 7- to 8-year-olds were reliable rotators [13]. Visual working memory holds about 3 items at 7 [18]. | Moderate |
| 9–10 | 9-year-olds built faster than 6-year-olds, made fewer errors and looked at the instructions less [10]. Visual working memory is about 3.8 items at 10 [18], close to the adult range of 3 to 5 [20]. | Moderate |

The evidence that building practice causes better maths is **mixed**. One classroom study found gains [8]. Another
study of 7- to 9-year-olds found better building times and some arithmetic gains, in the digital-training group only.
It found no gains in geometry or problem solving [9]. The link between building skill and maths is solid. Treat
"this app improves maths" as unproven and never put it in marketing.

### Pictures, screens and 3D models

- **Children struggle with typical picture instructions.** The problems found were colours that don't match the real
  parts, too much on one page ("cognitive overload"), attention tunnelling, bottom-to-top step order and complex
  graphic syntax (arrows, insets, callouts). The authors recommend simple graphics, accurate colours, clear angles of
  view and an order that matches how children like to assemble [10]. **Moderate.**
- **Screen to real objects is hard under 4.** In puzzle-copying studies, 2.5- to 3-year-olds learned less when they had
  to carry what they saw from a touchscreen to real pieces, or the other way [15]. 4- to 6-year-olds carried a solution
  from a touchscreen puzzle to the real puzzle. Children under 4 did so very little [17]. **Moderate.**
- **A social cue helps the youngest.** For toddlers, seeing a hand gesture with the moving pieces improved learning from
  a touchscreen [16]. In a separate study of LEGO tasks (learners' ages not checked), animations were slightly better
  than still pictures. Hiding the hands was better than showing them, and focusing on the parts that matter helped. A
  task learned on screen was done as well with real bricks as with virtual ones [11]. **Moderate, partly conflicting.**
  For Tile Builder: animate the new tile sliding into place. Mark where it goes with a pulsing outline. Don't draw
  realistic hands. For 3-year-olds, a grown-up pointing is the social cue.
- **Rotatable 3D instructions exist but are not studied with children.** LEGO's Builder app lets families zoom and turn
  the model and split the steps between builders [22]. No study was found that tests a rotatable 3D guide against
  pictures with children aged 3 to 10. **Gap.**

### Does turning the model help or confuse?

- Mental rotation emerges around 4, in simple 2D tasks [12]. 3D mental rotation is hard for most children under 7 [13].
  In infants, handling an object helps mental rotation more than watching it [14]. **Moderate.**
- **Inference.** A young child copies most easily when the screen shows the build from the same side they see the real
  one. Every turn of the model adds a mental rotation they can't yet do reliably. A model that keeps turning by itself
  during a step means the picture rarely matches the tiles. So for 3–5:
  - fix the view to the child's side while a step is on screen
  - show at most one slow turn when the step first appears, then stop
  - turn in 90° jumps with the ◀ ▶ buttons, and add a "back to my side" button

  From 6–8, turning is useful for seeing hidden sides, and the child should control it. At 9–10, free orbit is fine.
  This needs testing in Phase 4.

### How many tiles per step

No study tests "pieces per step" for children. The rule below is **inference** from three sources:

- Visual working memory is about 1.5 items at 5, 2.9 at 7 and 3.8 at 10 [18]. It grows from 3 to 5 [19]. Adults hold
  3 to 5 [20]. In those tasks, an "item" is a coloured square. A tile placement has more parts to remember: shape,
  colour, place and angle. So real limits are likely lower than these numbers.
- "Cognitive overload" was a main reason children failed with picture instructions [10].
- LEGO's own instructions have moved to 1 to 4 pieces per step, even in sets for older children, with a parts box on
  each step [21]. **Weak** (industry practice).

| Band | New tiles per step | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3–5 | **1** (2 only if they are the same shape and placed as a mirror pair, "one on each side") | The step's tile is shown as a picture and read aloud |
| 6–8 | **up to 3**, ideally one shape per step | Group by layer |
| 9–10 | **up to 4**, or one repeated group ("make 4 walls like this one") | Sub-assemblies allowed |

## 2. Guided play, free play and instructions

### What the evidence says

- **Guided play** sits between free play and direct instruction. An adult sets a learning goal and arranges the play.
  The child stays in control of the play itself [24][25]. **Definition.**
- **"Taking Shape"** (Fisher, Hirsh-Pasek, Newcombe and Golinkoff, 2013): 70 children aged 4 to 5 were taught the
  properties of four shapes through guided play, free play or didactic instruction. The guided-play group learned the
  shapes best, and the gain was still there a week later [23]. **Moderate** (one study, replicated in the meta-analysis
  below).
- **Meta-analysis** (Skene et al., 2022): 39 studies; 17 in the meta-analysis with 3,893 children aged 1 to 8. Guided
  play beat direct instruction on early maths (g = 0.24), shape knowledge (g = 0.63) and task switching (g = 0.40). It
  beat free play on spatial vocabulary (g = 0.93). How guided play was done varied a lot between studies [26]. **Strong.**
- **Instruction narrows exploration.** Preschoolers who were taught one function of a new toy played with it for less
  time and found fewer things it could do. Overhearing another child being taught had the same effect [27]. **Moderate.**
- **But not always.** A 2025 study found that when the adult invites the child to explore and does not demonstrate the
  answer, the teaching tone does not reduce exploration [28]. **Moderate.**
- **Copying a model has its own value.** 8-year-olds who did 5 sessions of structured block play (building from a
  model) improved mental rotation more than a board-game group [30]. In 43 children of about 8, copying block designs
  improved addition and subtraction. Free building from imagination did not [29]. **Moderate** (small samples).
- **Industry example.** Connetix's PRO "Build It 4 Ways" packs give four guided builds with step-by-step instructions.
  The intent is to "master the guided builds, then adapt, combine and reimagine" [31][32]. They are aimed at older
  children and advanced builders [32].

### Resolving step-by-step against creativity

Following steps is direct instruction. On its own it is the format that narrows exploration [27]. But copying a model
also builds spatial skill [29][30]. So the answer is a sequence, not a choice: **copy, then change, then invent**. The
app sets the goal, and the child controls the play, which is guided play [24]. After the copy, the app asks; it does not
show [27][28].

### The progression for Tile Builder

| Stage | What the kid does | 3–5 | 6–8 | 9–10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Copy** | Follows the steps | With a grown-up; 1 tile per step | Alone or with help; up to 3 per step | Alone; up to 4 per step |
| **Change** | Same build, one twist. The app asks a question and does not demonstrate | "Can you make it taller?" "Swap a colour." "Add a door." | "Make it twice as wide." "Turn the roof into a pyramid." | "Use only triangles for the roof." "Make it symmetrical." |
| **Build it another way** | Same tiles, a different thing (the Connetix idea) | — | "These tiles made a house. What else can they make?" | Same, plus "use exactly these 20 tiles" |
| **Challenge** | Sees only the finished model and builds it without steps. This is the structured copying in [29][30] | — | Small models (up to about 12 tiles), with turn buttons | Larger models, free orbit |
| **Invent** | Free build from a prompt, then a photo | "Build a home for your teddy." | Theme prompts ("a castle for a dragon") | Open prompts; kid names their build |

Every project card offers Change and Invent after the Copy. The app never says Copy is the "right" way.

## 3. Shapes, geometry and maths talk

### What the evidence says

- **Young children learn shape names from typical examples** (an equilateral triangle sitting on its base). They often
  reject unusual ones, such as a long thin triangle or one standing on its point. Children of 5 and under tend to leave
  non-standard examples out [34][35]. **Moderate** (from search summaries).
- **Guided play teaches what makes a shape a shape** (for example, a triangle has three sides) better than free play or
  telling, in 4- to 5-year-olds [23]. **Moderate.**
- **Recognising shapes and putting them together go with more complex builds.** In 66 preschoolers (mean age 4.7),
  shape recognition and shape composition predicted how complex children's block builds were. Mental rotation did not
  [6]. **Moderate.**
- **Talk matters.** Spatial toys, and the spatial words and gestures they draw from adults, shape early spatial skill
  [33]. Guided play beat free play on spatial vocabulary by a large margin (g = 0.93) [26]. **Moderate to strong.**
- **Magnet tiles make composition visible.** For example, two right triangles make a square [36]. **Weak** (practice
  source).

### What this means

Magnet tile sets are full of shapes that young children don't call "triangles": the tall isosceles triangle and the
right triangle. They are a natural way to stretch shape categories. The read-aloud should:

- name every shape by its category and its size: "a tall triangle. It's still a triangle: it has three sides."
- sometimes show shapes turned or on their point, so a triangle is not always point-up
- show composition: two right triangles make a square; two squares make a rectangle; four triangles over a square
  make a pyramid; six squares make a cube
- for 6+, show a **net** in the Change or Challenge stage: lay six squares flat in a cross, then fold them up into a
  cube (a design idea, not a research finding)
- ask questions as well as give commands: "How many sides does the roof tile have?" That is the guided-play move [23][26]

### Words the read-aloud can model

These are design choices, informed by [23][26][33]. Use the words naturally inside step instructions. Don't drill them.

| Band | Shape words | Space and position words | Number and pattern words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3–5 | square, triangle, tall triangle, rectangle, side, corner, flat, big, small | on top, under, next to, beside, inside, in front, behind, up, down, tall, short | count to 10, same, different, more, one more, pair |
| 6–8 | edge, face, cube, pyramid, prism, half, whole, right angle | left, right, opposite, between, turn, flip, row, layer, symmetrical ("the same on both sides") | count by 2s and 4s, pattern, repeat, twice as tall |
| 9–10 | net, vertex and vertices, equilateral, isosceles, parallel, line of symmetry | rotate a quarter turn, mirror image, top view, side view | count faces, edges and vertices; area as squares covered; halves and quarters of a square |

## 4. Rewards, stickers and praise

### What the evidence says

- **The overjustification effect.** In 1973, 51 preschoolers who drew pictures to earn an award they expected later
  drew less in free time. Children who got the same award unexpectedly, or no award, did not [37]. **Moderate** (one
  classic study).
- **Meta-analysis of 128 studies** (Deci, Koestner and Ryan, 1999). Rewards tied to doing a task, finishing it or doing
  it well all lowered free-choice interest (d = −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28). So did tangible rewards and expected rewards
  as groups. Unexpected rewards did not. Tangible rewards hurt children more than college students. Verbal rewards
  (positive feedback) helped children less than they helped students [38]. **Strong**, though contested: other
  researchers published critiques of how the meta-analysis was done [39], and the authors replied for education
  settings [40].
- **The AAP's 2026 policy** names "intermittent variable reward systems" among high-risk design features, along with
  endless scroll, heavy push notifications and usage-maximising algorithms [45][46]. **Official guidance** (read through
  secondary summaries).
- **Praise.** **UNVERIFIED in this session.** Research on praise suggests that praising the process ("you tried a new
  way") supports persistence better than praising the person ("you're so smart"). The usual studies are Mueller and
  Dweck (1998), Gunderson et al. (2013) and Cimpian et al. (2007). See
  [Not verified in this session](#not-verified-in-this-session). Treat this as likely but unchecked.

### Safe reward design for Tile Builder

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Make the **finished build and its photo** the reward | Promise a prize before a build ("Finish to get a sticker!"), since expected, completion-tied rewards undermine interest [37][38] |
| Give a short **celebration that is the same every time**, about the build: "You built the castle! Look how tall the tower is." | Use random or surprise prizes, loot-box reveals or "rare" stickers. These are variable rewards [45][46] |
| Treat the sticker as a **keepsake on the photo** that names what was built. Don't announce it before the build | Show sticker counts, collection grids, "3 of 30", unlocks, levels or points |
| Praise the process and describe what you see: "You found another way when it fell." (UNVERIFIED basis, low risk) | Praise the person: "You're a genius builder!" |
| Let the child choose the next build | Use streaks, timers, daily goals or "come back tomorrow" nudges (the plan already rules these out) |

## 5. Screens and hands

### What the guidance says

- **AAP 2016** ("Media and Young Minds"): for ages 2 to 5, limit screen use to 1 hour a day of high-quality programming.
  Co-view, help children understand what they see, and help them apply it to the world around them [47].
  **Official guidance.**
- **AAP 2026** ("Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents"; policy statement and technical report, released
  20 January 2026 and published in *Pediatrics*, February 2026) [41][42]. This was read through AAP news pages and
  secondary summaries only [43][44][45][46]. It:
  - moves the focus from screen-time limits alone to the wider "digital ecosystem" [43]
  - introduces the "5 Cs" for families: Child, Content, Calm, Crowding Out, Communication [44]
  - keeps no screens before 18 months, except video chat [44]
  - asks for safety and privacy by default: autoplay off, no targeted ads to minors, and fewer designs that prolong
    engagement [45]
  - names endless scroll, push notifications, intermittent variable rewards and usage-maximising algorithms as high-risk
    designs [45][46]
  - asks families to make a family media plan [45]

  **Unconfirmed:** whether the 1-hour guide for ages 2 to 5 still stands. One parenting blog says it does [48].
  Another says time caps were "retired" [49]. Neither is a reliable source, and the statement itself could not be read.
  Either way, short co-used sessions fit both readings.
- **For 6 and older**, the 2016 guidance asked for consistent limits rather than a set number of hours.
  **UNVERIFIED** (from memory of the 2016 statement, not re-checked).
- **Screens and solitary play.** **UNVERIFIED in this session.** Studies of parents with toddlers suggest that
  electronic toys and tablet books cut parent talk compared with traditional toys and print books. They also suggest a
  tablet pulls the child's body and attention away from the shared space. The usual studies are Sosa (2016) and Munzer
  et al. (2019). See [Not verified in this session](#not-verified-in-this-session).

### How the app should hand off from screen to hands

The goal: the tiles are the activity, and the iPad is a recipe card.

1. **Propped up, not in the lap.** Before the first build, show a card for the grown-up: "Stand the iPad up beside the
   tiles, where you can both see it." This keeps the build in the shared space. (Inference; the UNVERIFIED
   solitary-positioning research would support it.)
2. **One glance per step.** Each step screen holds one picture, one sentence read aloud and one Next button. The voice
   carries the instruction, so eyes can stay on the tiles.
3. **Short screen moments.** Projects for 3–5 are small (see the table below). The screen dims to a calm "keep
   building" state between taps rather than adding anything to watch. The app adds nothing to stretch the session: no
   autoplay to the next project, no "one more?" prompt [45].
4. **Co-use by design for the youngest.** The 3–5 read-aloud speaks to both people at times: "Grown-up, hold the wall
   while your builder adds the roof." This matches the AAP's co-viewing advice [47] and the evidence that children under
   4 need a social bridge from screen to objects [15][16][17].
5. **End on the real thing.** The last screen is the camera pointed at the real build, then "Put the iPad down and play
   with what you made."

## 6. Attention, session length and when a build fails

### Attention and session length

**No evidence was checked in this session.** A rule of thumb ("a few minutes of focus per year of age") circulates in
parenting media. No primary source for it was found, so it should not set the numbers. The proposal below is
**inference** from working memory [18], from the instruction-overload finding [10] and from the AAP's advice against
engagement-prolonging design [45]:

- every project can be finished in one sitting
- big projects for 9–10 come in chapters, and each chapter ends with a stable build that can be photographed
- leaving mid-build saves the step silently, with no nagging to come back

Phase 4 testing should time real builds by age and replace these guesses.

### When a build falls down

- Children aged 4 to 8 naturally build **layer by layer**, which gives stable in-between states [4]. Picture
  instructions that run against children's natural assembly order caused errors [10]. So steps should go up layer by
  layer. **Suggested checker rule** (inference): every in-between state must stand on its own. For example, no
  single free-standing wall that is taller than it is wide before the walls next to it are added.
- What the app says after a collapse (inference, with an UNVERIFIED basis in the praise research):
  1. Normalise it: "Towers fall sometimes. Builders fix them."
  2. Offer two big buttons: "Go back one step" and "Start this layer again".
  3. After two falls on the same step, suggest a grown-up: "Ask a grown-up to hold it while you add the next tile."
  4. Never show a failure sound, a red X or a score.
- What it says when a tile is missing: the matching engine's swaps come first ("Use two short triangles instead").
  If there's no swap, "Make it your way" opens the Change stage. That turns the gap into a creative prompt.

## 7. Boys and girls

### What the evidence says

- In the picture-instruction study, 6-year-old girls placed the fewest parts correctly, and 9-year-old girls enjoyed it
  least [10]. **Weak** (24 children). It suggests some instruction styles serve girls less well, perhaps because of
  past experience, not ability.
- Several studies found that gender interacts with whether instructions are animated or still [51][52]. Details were not
  checked. **Weak.**
- In preschoolers, a shape-recognition-by-gender interaction predicted block-build complexity [6]. **Weak.**
- A 2023 study is titled "Mental rotation is supported by block play in boys and girls" [50]. Only the title was seen.
- Income matters too: children from lower-income homes are behind in spatial skill by 3 [2], and building skill
  relates to number skill most in lower-income children [5].
- **UNVERIFIED in this session**: "Building a Pink Dinosaur" (Fulcher and Hayes, 2018, on pink and feminine-coded
  versus masculine-coded construction sets); the Let Toys Be Toys campaign; and the Geena Davis Institute's research
  on gender and play. See [Not verified in this session](#not-verified-in-this-session).

### What to do

These are already in the plan: no "boys'" and "girls'" sections, and a palette that comes from the tiles. Add:

- **Themes for everyone, in every age band.** Castles, animals, vehicles, space, gardens and fantasy all appear in all
  three bands. No theme is drawn in a gender-coded way. Dragons are not just for boys, and flowers are not just for
  girls.
- **Profile pictures** are animals, objects and shapes, not boys and girls.
- **Neutral words.** Use "builder" and "you"; avoid "guys". Don't attach "pretty" to some builds and "cool" or "strong"
  to others.
- **Test with both.** In Phase 4, track completion and enjoyment for girls and boys separately. Check whether the 3D
  steps work as well for both, given [10].
- **Small sets count.** Many 3–5 projects should be buildable with a 32-piece set. That helps families who can't buy a
  big set, the group with the most to gain [2][5].

### Changes this suggests to `PLAN.md` (for Jordan to decide)

1. **Phase 4 "done when".** "A 3- or 4-year-old finishes a build with only the app's help" is ambitious for a
   3-year-old. The evidence puts the floor for copying at about 4 [4], and screen-to-hands transfer is weak under 4
   [15][17]. Suggested wording: "A 4- or 5-year-old finishes a 1-tile-per-step build with only the app's help. A
   3-year-old finishes one with a grown-up."
2. **Build mode.** "It also turns slowly by itself" should not apply while a 3–5 step is on screen (see rotation
   controls above).
3. **Library and project data.** Add Change prompts and an Invent prompt to every project. Add a model-only Challenge
   for 6+.
4. **My Builds.** Keep the sticker, but as an unannounced keepsake with no counts or collections.
5. **Checker.** Add "steps go up layer by layer" and "every in-between state stands".
6. **Parent side.** Add a first-run card: "Stand the iPad up beside the tiles and build together."
