# Character appeal

Jordan's verdict on a round that added eyelids, cheek bulges, nostrils, gill fronds and a prop: "they are uglier now."
Every addition competed with the face. This skill exists so that doesn't happen again.

## The rules (in order of weight)

1. **Subtract first.** Before adding anything, ask what to take away. A detail earns its place only if the character
   reads worse without it. Pixar's *Ratatouille* team kept "simple direct shapes" and even froze the nose so nothing
   distracted from the eyes.
2. **One silhouette, readable as a black shape at thumbnail size.** No fussy, broken contours: no bumps, spikes,
   fronds, or bits that poke out unless they *are* the character's one idea.
3. **One idea per character.** The snail's idea is the tile shell; the dino's is the tile plates; the axolotl's is
   its gills. Everything else stays plain.
4. **Faces are the whole game: minimal.** Two eyes, a tiny mouth, maybe blush. Kawaii faces: eyes wide-set and low,
   the mouth close under them, any nose level with the eyes. No lids, brows, nostrils, cheek lumps, iris rings or
   streaks unless the character is ruined without them.
5. **Eyes: simple, dark, glossy.** A near-black oval with one big and one small catch-light beats a detailed iris.
   Big eyes read innocent; any droop or lid reads tired, sad or sly.
6. **Mouth: small.** A short smile or a small open "D". Big open mouths read as shouting or eating.
7. **Round shapes say friendly.** Soft, full forms; a low centre of gravity (bottom-heavy pear) reads soft and
   stable. Sharp triangles read energetic or dangerous: tiles on a character must be few, small, and placed where
   they read as decoration, never as spikes.
8. **Contrast in sizes.** Big head, small body; one big shape, one medium, one small. Not everything the same size.
9. **Nothing near the mouth.** Props in front of the face read as eating it.
10. **Colour: one main colour, one light accent (belly), one tile accent.** Muddy pastels look cheap; keep the main
    colour clean and saturated enough to read on the wood table.

## The critique loop (every render, before it is shown)

1. Look at it at thumbnail size (about 200 px). Does each character read in one glance? Is the face the first thing
   you see?
2. Squint: does the silhouette read as one clean shape?
3. Name every detail on the face and body. For each: would it be missed if it were gone? If not, remove it.
4. Read the expression in words (happy, curious, shy). If the word is sad, sly, tired, angry or scary, find the
   cause (usually lids, brows or a big mouth) and remove it.
5. Compare with the previous round side by side. If the new one isn't clearly better, revert the change.
6. Only then send it, with an honest note of what is still weak.
